Act 1 • Core Concept

What is an Incident?

An unplanned interruption or reduction in quality of an IT service.

In simple terms:

Think of a building's fire alarm system.

Incident = the fire alarm goes off. Something is wrong right now. Drop everything, deal with it immediately, restore normal as fast as possible. Goal: put the fire out.

Problem = the fire investigation afterward. Why did the fire start? What can we change so it never happens again? Goal: prevent the next fire.
ITIL distinction: Incident Management restores service. Problem Management prevents future incidents. They are separate practices that work together — Lab 14 covers Problem Management.

Act 1 • Core Concept

Incident vs Problem: side by side

Incident (this lab)

  • Something is broken right now
  • Goal: restore service as fast as possible
  • Reactive — triggered by alert or user report
  • Example: "UPI payments failing at 80% rate"
  • Measure: MTTR (Mean Time to Resolve)

Problem (Lab 14)

  • Something keeps breaking
  • Goal: find root cause, prevent recurrence
  • Proactive — opened after pattern detected
  • Example: "WHY do UPI payments keep failing?"
  • Measure: reduction in recurring incidents
Key rule: When a P1 fires at 2 AM, you do NOT stop to investigate the root cause. You restore service first (Incident). You investigate why it happened the next morning (Problem). Speed vs thoroughness — two separate jobs.

Act 1 • Priority

Impact and Urgency: what do they mean?

In simple terms:

Imagine a burst pipe in a building.

Impact = how many rooms are flooded. One bathroom = low impact. The entire ground floor = high impact.

Urgency = how fast is it spreading. Slow drip you can deal with tomorrow = low urgency. Gushing water reaching the server room in 10 minutes = high urgency.

The Priority Matrix: Impact x Urgency

Priority is auto-calculated in ServiceNow. You set Impact and Urgency; the system determines Priority.

                         Impact
                  1-High    2-Medium    3-Low
Urgency  1-High    P1          P2        P3
         2-Medium  P2          P3        P4
         3-Low     P3          P4        P5

Priority:
  P1 = Critical    "All UPI payments down"          SLA: 1 hour
  P2 = High        "Payment gateway degraded"       SLA: 4 hours
  P3 = Moderate    "Settlement batch slow"           SLA: 8 hours
  P4 = Low         "Single user printer issue"       SLA: 24 hours
  P5 = Planning    "UI font looks wrong"             SLA: 72 hours
Why not just set Priority directly? Because people disagree. One manager says everything is P1. Another underreports to avoid scrutiny. The matrix removes subjectivity — Impact and Urgency are facts you observe; Priority becomes the logical result.

Act 2 • Lifecycle

Full incident lifecycle

New Incident created. SLA clock starts. Awaiting assignment.
In Progress Assigned to agent. Investigation begins. Work notes added.
On Hold Waiting for vendor, user, or external input. SLA clock pauses.
In Progress Response received. Work resumes. SLA clock resumes.
Resolved Fix applied. Resolution code + notes documented. User notified.
Closed User confirms fix. Close code + notes. Incident complete.
Practice this now: Open the VPN P1 incident. Walk it through every state. At each transition, add a work note explaining what happened. Review the Activity Stream at the end — that is your audit trail.

Act 2 • SLA

Why the SLA clock matters

In simple terms:

Imagine ordering pizza and being told "delivery in 30 minutes." The SLA clock is that 30-minute timer. If the pizza arrives in 45 minutes, the delivery is breached — consequences follow (discount, complaint, lost customer).

For a P1 IT incident affecting 10 million UPI users, that timer is 60 minutes. Every minute over that is money lost, reputation damaged, and possibly an RBI compliance violation.
  • Without an SLA clock, nobody has a deadline — and work expands to fill all available time
  • The clock creates urgency and accountability: the agent knows exactly how much time is left
  • When the clock turns red, managers are automatically notified — no human needed to escalate
  • The clock is the difference between "we'll look into it" and "we have 40 minutes left"

Act 2 • Escalation

What is escalation?

In simple terms:

The junior doctor in A&E tries to treat the patient. The problem is beyond their skill — they call the specialist (functional escalation). Meanwhile the patient is deteriorating fast and the specialist is not responding — the head of department is paged (hierarchical escalation).

Escalation is not a failure. It is the correct process for getting the right people involved at the right time.
TypeWhen to use itWhat you actually do in ServiceNow
FunctionalThe current team does not have the skills to fix it. Need a specialist.Change Assignment Group to the specialist team. Reassignment count increments.
HierarchicalSLA is about to breach. Management awareness needed. Business impact growing.Notify manager. Flag the incident. Escalation checkbox ticked.

Act 2 • Link

Why linking records matters

In simple terms:

Imagine a hospital where every doctor treats a patient in isolation — no shared medical record, no history, no connection between visits. The same tests are run repeatedly. The same drug interactions are missed. The same diagnosis is made wrong three times.

Linking records in ServiceNow is the shared medical record. When you attach a CI, a KB article, a Problem, or a Change to an incident, every future person working that CI sees the full history. Pattern recognition becomes automatic.
  • Attach the CI → see every other incident on that same server this month
  • Attach a Problem → this incident is a symptom of a known root cause
  • Attach a KB article → the fix is already documented; MTTR drops
  • Attach a Change → "this incident was caused by last night's deployment"

Act 2 • Major Incident

What makes an incident "major"?

In simple terms:

A burst pipe in one office bathroom = a normal incident. One team fixes it quietly.

A burst main that floods the entire building, knocks out the data centre on the ground floor, and leaves 500 staff unable to work = a major incident. It needs a dedicated incident commander, multiple teams working in parallel, regular status updates, and a post-incident review.
TriggerExample
P1 affecting a critical business serviceAll UPI payment processing is down
Multiple systems impacted simultaneouslyGateway + DB + Load Balancer all failing
SLA already breached with no end in sightP1 open for 90 minutes, no resolution path
Executive / regulatory visibility requiredRBI, NPCI, or media attention involved
Key process: A major incident is not just a "big" incident. It triggers a separate communication process: dedicated commander, bridge call, 15-minute status cadence, executive updates.

Act 2 • Automation

What are assignment rules?

In simple terms:

Imagine a hospital receptionist who, every time a patient walks in, has to manually figure out which department to send them to. "Are you here for a broken arm? Okay, let me think... that is probably orthopaedics..." This takes time and introduces human error.

Assignment rules are the automatic triage system: "Broken arm? Orthopaedics. Chest pain? Cardiology. Immediately, every time, no thinking required." In ServiceNow: "Category = Network? Assign to Network group. Done."
Why this matters: Without assignment rules, every incident sits in a queue until a human manually assigns it. That is wasted minutes on a P1. With assignment rules, the incident lands directly with the right team the moment it is created.
