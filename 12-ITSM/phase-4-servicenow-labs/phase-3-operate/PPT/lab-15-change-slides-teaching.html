Act 1 • Three Types of Change

Not all changes are equal

In simple terms: ITIL recognises that "change" covers a huge range -- from renewing an SSL cert in an emergency to a carefully planned database migration. Treating them all the same would either slow everything down or let risky changes slip through. So ITIL defines three types, each with the right level of control.
Normal Change -- Something new, risky, or never done before. Needs full planning + a board of experts to approve it. Takes days to weeks.
Standard Change -- A routine task done many times before. Pre-approved by policy. Can be done in minutes with no extra sign-off.
Emergency Change -- Production is on fire right now. Fix first, fill in the paperwork second. Still needs verbal approval -- no change is truly "no approval".

Act 1 • Decision Framework

What are the 7 Rs?

In simple terms: The 7 Rs are 7 questions you must answer before any Normal Change is approved. Think of them as a checklist a doctor runs through before an operation -- Who is the patient? Why is surgery needed? What is the expected outcome? What can go wrong? The CAB will not approve a change until all 7 are answered.
  • R1 -- RAISED: Who asked for this change?
  • R2 -- REASON: Why is it needed?
  • R3 -- RETURN: What benefit do we expect?
  • R4 -- RISKS: What could go wrong?
  • R5 -- RESOURCES: What do we need (people, time, servers)?
  • R6 -- RESPONSIBLE: Who does the work and who approves?
  • R7 -- RELATIONSHIP: What other changes or systems depend on this?
ITIL 4: Skipping even one R is how outages happen. If you can't answer "what are the risks" (R4), the change is not ready to be approved -- go back and do the analysis.

Act 2 • Normal Change Planning

The Implementation Plan

In simple terms: The Implementation Plan is a step-by-step script for the night of the change. It reads like a recipe with exact times. If you don't have a plan this specific, the CAB will send you back to write one. Vague plans get rejected.
1. Pre-implementation (2:00 AM):
   - Notify ops team, take full config backup
   - Verify staging tests passed (sign-off from QA)

2. Deploy UPI Transaction Service v2.1 (2:15 AM):
   - Spring Security enabled on /chaos/* endpoints
   - Verify: /chaos returns 401 without auth token
   - Verify: /api/upi/pay still processes normally

3. Deploy UPI Settlement Service v2.1 (2:45 AM):
   - Resilience4j circuit breaker activated
   - Verify: circuit breaker metrics visible in Prometheus dashboard

4. Smoke test (3:00 AM):
   - Run 100 test transactions
   - Success threshold: 100% pass rate

5. Chaos test (3:15 AM):
   - Verify /chaos/enable without auth returns 401
   - Verify circuit breaker opens and recovers on 5 failures

6. Monitoring phase (3:30 AM - 5:00 AM):
   - Watch Grafana dashboard: all panels must stay green
   - On-call engineer stays available for 90 minutes

Act 2 • Normal Change Planning

The Rollback Plan

In simple terms: A Rollback Plan is the emergency exit. Before you start any change, you must know exactly how to undo it. If you can't describe the rollback in clear steps, you are not ready to make the change. The CAB will reject a change with no rollback plan -- and rightly so.

If anything fails, execute this:

1. Stop both services immediately
2. Revert to v2.0 Docker images
3. Run: docker compose up -d
4. Verify both services are healthy
5. Re-apply original workaround
6. Notify operations team
7. Schedule post-mortem for next morning

Estimated rollback time: 15 minutes

Test Plan (proof it works before go-live)

- Tested in staging for 5 days
- Spring Security: /chaos/* blocked
- Circuit breaker: opens after
  5 failures, half-opens after 30s
- P95 latency: unchanged vs v2.0
- JVM heap: stable under load
Rule of thumb: Rollback time should be shorter than the change window. If it takes 3 hours to apply but 3 hours to roll back, your window is too tight.

Act 2 • CAB

What is the CAB?

In simple terms: The CAB (Change Advisory Board) is like a review panel -- a group of experts who sit together (or meet virtually) to ask hard questions about a proposed change before it is allowed to go to production. Think of it as a gate that only opens when enough smart people have agreed the risk is acceptable.

Who is on the CAB?

  • Service owners (who know what could break)
  • Architects (who understand dependencies)
  • Security team (who check for vulnerabilities)
  • Operations (who manage the maintenance window)
  • Business representatives (for high-impact changes)

What does the CAB review?

  • Are all 7 Rs answered?
  • Is the rollback plan realistic?
  • Is the maintenance window approved?
  • Are the right CIs in the impact list?
  • Is there any conflict with other scheduled changes?
Result: CAB votes Approve or Reject. Only after approval does the change move to Scheduled state.

Why CMDB Matters for Changes

In simple terms: Before you change anything, you need to know what else will be affected. The CMDB is a map of all your systems and how they connect. Without it, you are driving with no headlights -- you change one service and accidentally break three others. The CI Impact Analysis reads that map automatically.

Without CMDB

  • CAB has to rely on tribal knowledge
  • Easy to miss a dependent service
  • Impact list is incomplete = surprise outages
  • Post-incident: "nobody knew that service depended on this"

With CMDB (Lab 07)

  • Click "CI Impact" and the relationships appear instantly
  • Every dependency is documented and visible
  • CAB can make an informed decision
  • No surprises at 2 AM during implementation
The CMDB you built in Lab 07 pays off here. Every CI relationship you mapped is now working for you -- the system tells you what to verify before you start.

Act 2 • Emergency Change

When should you raise an Emergency Change?

In simple terms: An Emergency Change is for situations where waiting even one hour for a Normal Change process would cause significant, ongoing harm. It is NOT for convenience or impatience. If production is not actively broken, you should use a Normal Change.

USE Emergency Change when:

  • A P1 incident is active and revenue is being lost every minute
  • A security vulnerability is being actively exploited
  • Regulatory-mandated fix must be in place within hours
  • Agreed with VP / CTO verbally to proceed

DO NOT use Emergency Change when:

  • You forgot to raise the Normal Change on time
  • The change is urgent but not causing active harm right now
  • You want to skip the CAB because it's inconvenient
  • It's a standard change that could be pre-approved
Misusing Emergency Changes is itself a risk. If a team routinely raises Emergency Changes to skip process, it indicates a broken change culture -- and becomes a Problem record to investigate.

What is a Blackout Window?

In simple terms: A Blackout Window is a time period when the business has said: "No changes to production, period." The reason is always the same -- during these periods the system is under maximum stress or scrutiny, and even a small change going wrong would be catastrophic. It's like a "do not disturb" sign for your entire infrastructure.

Act 3 • When Things Go Wrong

Why a failed change is not a failure

In simple terms: Many engineers feel embarrassed when a change has to be rolled back. But ITIL sees it differently. A rolled-back change with no service disruption is proof that the process worked. The rollback plan you wrote saved the day. A failed change that causes an outage happens when there is NO rollback plan -- that is the real failure.

What failure looks like WITHOUT process

  • Change goes wrong at 2 AM
  • No rollback plan exists
  • Team scrambles for hours
  • P1 incident raised
  • 350+ banks affected
  • RBI investigation triggered

What failure looks like WITH process

  • Change goes wrong at 2 AM
  • Rollback plan is executed (15 minutes)
  • Workaround re-applied
  • Close code: Unsuccessful
  • Post-mortem scheduled
  • Root cause fixed, change rescheduled
The outcome of the second scenario: Zero service disruption. Zero incidents. The process absorbed the failure before it became a problem. This is exactly what Change Management is designed to do.
