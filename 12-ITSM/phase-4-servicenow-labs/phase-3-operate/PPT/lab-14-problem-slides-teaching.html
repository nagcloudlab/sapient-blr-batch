Teaching Note • In Simple Terms

Why the aspirin analogy matters

In simple terms: Every time Meera gets a headache alert, she takes aspirin (fixes the incident). The headache goes away. But it comes back next week. A Problem Manager asks: "Why do you keep getting headaches?" The answer might be: "You need glasses." That is the root cause — and fixing it means the headache never returns.

Incident Management world

  • Alert fires → engineer wakes up
  • Restarts the service → alert clears
  • Goes back to sleep
  • Same alert fires next week
  • Cycle repeats forever

Problem Management world

  • Notices: this has happened 4 times
  • Opens a Problem record
  • Investigates the actual cause
  • Documents it + a workaround
  • Fixes it once, permanently
The business value: Without Problem Management, teams spend all their time fighting fires. With it, they stop fires from starting in the first place. This is the shift from reactive IT to proactive IT.

Teaching Note • In Simple Terms

How Incidents, Problems and Changes connect

In simple terms: Think of it like a leaking roof. The incident is each bucket you place to catch drips. The problem is investigating why the roof leaks. The Known Error is writing down "the roof leaks when it rains hard because a tile is cracked". The Change Request is hiring someone to replace the tile permanently.
1
Incidents are the buckets — quick reactions that stop immediate pain. Multiple incidents can share the same root cause.
2
Problem is the investigation — "why does the roof keep leaking?" It groups all related incidents together.
3
Known Error is the written-down knowledge — "we know the cause, here is how to work around it until it is fixed."
4
Change Request is the permanent repair — requires approval, planning, and controlled deployment.

Teaching Note • What Is RCA

What is Root Cause Analysis?

In simple terms: Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a structured way of asking "what really caused this?" It goes deeper than the surface symptom to find the underlying reason so the same thing cannot happen again.

Without RCA

  • Service fails → restart it
  • Feels solved... until next time
  • Team burned out from repeat alerts
  • No one knows why it really failed
  • No improvement over time

With RCA

  • Service fails → restart it (Incident)
  • Then ask: why did it fail?
  • Dig into logs, metrics, code
  • Find the actual design flaw
  • Fix it once — no more recurrence
RCA is not about blame. It is about finding the system or process weakness that allowed the failure to happen. The question is not "who broke it?" but "what in our design, process, or monitoring allowed this to go undetected?"

Teaching Note • The 5 Whys Technique

What is the 5 Whys technique?

In simple terms: The 5 Whys is the simplest RCA technique. You start with the symptom and keep asking "but WHY did that happen?" until you cannot go deeper. Usually 5 levels is enough to reach the true root cause.

Example from everyday life (before the UPI example):

Problem: The car won't start.
Why 1: The battery is dead. → Why 2: The alternator failed. → Why 3: The alternator belt broke. → Why 4: The belt was worn and was never replaced. → Why 5: The car was never serviced on schedule.

Root Cause: No preventive maintenance schedule. Fix = set up a maintenance calendar. If you stopped at Why 1 (replace battery), the new battery would also die soon.
Key rule: Do not stop at the first answer. Each answer becomes the next question. Keep going until you hit a process, design, or policy gap — something you can actually fix permanently.

Act 2 • RCA

Root Cause Analysis: the 5 Whys

WHY #1: Why did UPI payments fail?
        --> Transaction failure rate spiked from 0% to 80% instantaneously.

WHY #2: Why did it spike instantaneously (not gradually)?
        --> Something toggled the service into a failure state externally.
            Not resource exhaustion (JVM heap at 45%, latency normal).

WHY #3: Why could something toggle the service externally?
        --> The service exposes /chaos/enable, /chaos/down, /chaos/latency
            endpoints with NO authentication.

WHY #4: Why are chaos endpoints exposed without authentication?
        --> They were added for testing and never secured for production.
            No code review caught the missing auth.

WHY #5: Why did settlements also fail?
        --> No circuit breaker between Settlement and Transaction services.
            Failures cascade with no fallback or timeout.
Root Cause: Unprotected chaos engineering endpoints + missing circuit breaker pattern. Any network-reachable client can trigger a production outage.

Act 2 • Document (1 of 3)

Document findings: Cause Notes

On the Problem form, fill in the Analysis Information tab. Start with the Cause Notes section.

In simple terms: Cause Notes answer the question: "What exactly is broken, and why?" Be precise. A vague cause note like "service was unstable" is useless. A good cause note names the specific technical flaw.

Cause Notes to document

  • /chaos/* endpoints have no authentication or authorisation
  • Any network-reachable client can call /chaos/enable and trigger a production outage
  • No circuit breaker exists between Settlement Service and Transaction Service
  • When Transaction Service fails, Settlement Service has no fallback — failures cascade
  • No network segmentation restricts access to chaos endpoints
  • Chaos endpoints were added for testing and were never reviewed or secured before production
Why document this carefully? If the same issue appears in 6 months, the new engineer can read the Cause Notes and immediately understand what happened without re-investigating from scratch.
Lab 14 • Cause NotesTeaching Edition • 11
Act 2 • Document (2 of 3)

Document findings: Workaround

In simple terms: The workaround is the "quick plaster" — it stops the bleeding right now but is not the permanent cure. It must be fast, reliable, and clear enough that anyone on the team can follow it at 3am during an outage.

Workaround steps

  • Run curl -X POST http://<service-host>:8080/chaos/disable
  • Service should recover within approximately 30 seconds
  • If service is unresponsive: run docker compose restart upi-transaction-service
  • Monitor for 15 minutes to confirm stability before closing the incident
Important distinction: A workaround resolves the incident (service is back up) but the Problem stays open. The Problem is only closed once the permanent fix is deployed and verified — which happens through a Change Request in Lab 15.
Lab 14 • WorkaroundTeaching Edition • 12
Act 2 • Document (3 of 3)

Document findings: Fix Notes (permanent)

In simple terms: Fix Notes describe what needs to change permanently so this problem never happens again. Unlike the workaround, the permanent fix requires proper planning, testing, and approval through a Change Request.

Permanent fix actions

  • SECURITY: Add Spring Security on /chaos/* endpoints — restrict to admin role only
  • RESILIENCE: Implement Resilience4j circuit breaker in Settlement Service to prevent cascade failures
  • RATE LIMIT: Add rate limiter on /api/upi/pay to prevent abuse
  • MONITORING: Add alerts for circuit breaker state changes (open/closed/half-open)
  • NETWORK: Restrict /chaos/* endpoints to internal management network only
Permanent fix requires a Change Request — it will be submitted and approved in Lab 15. This is intentional: permanent changes to production must go through CAB approval to prevent new problems from being introduced.

Teaching Note • What Is KEDB

What is the Known Error Database (KEDB)?

In simple terms: The KEDB is like a FAQ for IT agents. Whenever a known problem recurs, agents do not need to investigate from scratch. They search the KEDB, find the matching entry, apply the documented workaround, and resolve the incident in minutes instead of hours.

Without KEDB

  • New agent gets the alert at 2am
  • Has no idea what caused this before
  • Spends 2 hours investigating
  • Eventually finds the same fix
  • Resolution time: 2-3 hours

With KEDB

  • New agent gets the alert at 2am
  • Searches KEDB for "UPI failure"
  • Finds the Known Error article
  • Runs curl .../chaos/disable
  • Resolution time: 5-10 minutes
KEDB = institutional memory. Without it, every agent rediscovers the same root cause. With it, resolution time drops from hours to minutes. This directly lowers your MTTR (Mean Time to Resolve) metric.

Act 2 • Lifecycle

Problem lifecycle

In simple terms: A Problem has its own journey from "we noticed a pattern" to "we have permanently fixed it." Unlike an incident (which closes in hours), a Problem can stay open for weeks while the permanent fix is planned and deployed.
New Problem created from recurring incident pattern. Investigation not started.
Assess Investigation plan documented. Reviewing metrics, logs, code.
RCA Root Cause Analysis in progress. Work notes added with findings.
Known Error Root cause identified. Workaround documented. KEDB article published.
Fix in Progress Change Request submitted. Awaiting CAB approval.
Resolved / Closed Fix deployed and verified. No recurrence over 72 hours.
Key difference from incidents: A Problem stays open as a Known Error until the permanent fix is deployed and verified. It can take weeks. The workaround keeps the service running in the meantime.

Teaching Note • Proactive Problem Management

What is Proactive Problem Management?

In simple terms: Reactive Problem Management waits for incidents to pile up before investigating. Proactive Problem Management scans the CMDB, architecture diagrams, and monitoring data to find weaknesses before they ever cause an incident. It is the difference between finding a crack in the bridge before it collapses versus after.

Reactive (standard)

  • Incident occurs → service is down
  • Users are already affected
  • Multiple incidents pile up
  • Pattern detected → Problem opened
  • Damage has already happened

Proactive (mature)

  • No incidents yet
  • CMDB review finds a design risk
  • Problem opened before any failure
  • Fix deployed before users are affected
  • Zero downtime, zero disruption
Proactive Problem Management is the highest-maturity ITSM practice. It requires good CMDB data (Lab 07), good monitoring (Lab 12), and engineers with time to look beyond today's fires. Most teams only reach this level after mastering the reactive process first.
