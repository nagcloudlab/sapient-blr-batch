Act 1 • The Distinction

Change vs Release vs Deployment

Three different practices. Three different questions.

ConceptScopeQuestion It AnswersUPI Example
Change Single unit of modification WHAT can we change? "Upgrade PostgreSQL 14 to 15"
Release Collection of changes, tested and deployed together WHEN and HOW do we group changes? "UPI Platform v3.2.0" bundling 4 changes
Deployment Physical act of moving a release to an environment HOW do we technically push it? Rolling restart, DB migration, LB config
Why not deploy one by one? 4 individual deployments = 4 maintenance windows (4 Sundays), 4 separate risk windows, 4 times NOC stays up all night, and no integration testing between changes. A release bundles them, tests them together, deploys in one coordinated window.

Act 1 • In Simple Terms

The Music Album Analogy

These three concepts confuse a lot of people. Here is a simple analogy that makes it click.

Change = Individual Song

Each song is a self-contained unit of creative work. In ITSM, each change (CHG001, CHG002...) is a self-contained unit of technical modification. It has its own approval, its own risk, its own rollback.

Release = The Album

An album bundles multiple songs that belong together. They are recorded at the same time, tested for flow, and released as a coherent whole. A release bundles multiple changes that belong together and have been integration-tested.

Deployment = Pressing CDs and Shipping to Stores

This is the physical act of getting the album into the world. In ITSM, deployment is the technical act of moving the release to the production environment -- the rolling restart, the DB migration, the load balancer config.

Instructor note: Ask the class: "Can you deploy without a release? Can you have a release without deploying it?" Both answers are yes -- deployment is separate from the release record. A release can sit approved but undeployed. A hotfix can be deployed directly without a formal release record (though that is bad practice).

Act 1 • In Simple Terms

Versioning: What Do the Numbers Mean?

Version numbers like v3.2.1.1 follow a pattern. Each number tells you exactly how big the change is.

  v  3  .  2  .  1  .  1
     |     |     |     |
  MAJOR  MINOR PATCH HOTFIX

  MAJOR:  Big new features or architecture changes (full CAB, full testing)
  MINOR:  Small improvements and bug fixes (streamlined approval)
  PATCH:  Single focused fix (expedited approval)
  HOTFIX: Emergency -- bypass gates, review AFTER deployment
Major version jumps from v3 to v4 mean something fundamental changed -- like rebuilding a road vs repaving it. High risk, high reward. CAB mandatory.
Instructor note: Ask: "Why does Emergency bypass testing gates?" The answer is that production is already broken. The risk of doing nothing outweighs the risk of an untested fix. But you must do the post-review to close the loop -- otherwise you accumulate technical debt and ungoverned changes.

Act 1 • How to Deploy

4 Deployment Models

Not all deployments are equal. The method of deployment determines how much risk you take on and how fast you can recover.

Big-Bang

Switch everything at once. High risk. Simple to execute. Works for small tightly coupled systems where you cannot run two versions at the same time.

Rolling

Update servers one at a time. Some servers run v3.1, some run v3.2 simultaneously during the window. Medium risk. No extra infrastructure needed.

Canary (Our Strategy)

Send a tiny slice of real traffic to the new version first. Monitor. Gradually increase. Lowest risk for live traffic systems like UPI.

Blue-Green

Run two complete environments. Switch traffic atomically in one instant. Instant rollback. Requires double infrastructure cost.
Our strategy: Canary for UPI Transaction Service (gradual traffic shift). Blue-Green for UPI Settlement Service (batch processing -- switch atomically).

Act 1 • Deployment Models: Part 1

Big-Bang and Rolling Deployments

Big-Bang

Old v3.1 --> New v3.2 (all at once)
Risk: HIGH | Rollback: Full revert
Use: Small, tightly coupled systems
In simple terms: Imagine turning off all the lights in a building at once and switching to a new bulb type. If the new bulb type does not work, the whole building is dark until you swap back. Simple plan, maximum blast radius.

Rolling

Server 1: v3.2  (done)
Server 2: v3.1 --> v3.2  (in progress)
Server 3: v3.1  (waiting)
Server 4: v3.1  (waiting)
Risk: MEDIUM | Rollback: Stop + revert
In simple terms: You change the bulbs room by room. If one room goes dark, you stop and fix it. The rest of the building still has light. Slower but safer. The risk: during the window, two versions are live simultaneously -- you must ensure they are compatible.
Instructor note: "Why can't we always use rolling?" Ask the class. Key answer: if v3.2 has a DB schema change that is not backwards-compatible, then v3.1 servers pointing at the updated DB will break. You need to sequence the DB migration carefully when using rolling deployments.

Act 1 • Deployment Models: Part 2

Canary and Blue-Green Deployments

Canary (Our Strategy for Transaction Service)

95% traffic --> v3.1 (stable)
 5% traffic --> v3.2 (canary)
Monitor --> if OK --> ramp up
Risk: LOW | Rollback: Redirect traffic
In simple terms: Before serving a new dish at a restaurant, the chef tastes a small portion first. If it tastes right, serve it to the whole table. If something is wrong, you fix it before 100% of customers get the bad dish. Only 5% of UPI users are "test tasters."

Blue-Green (Our Strategy for Settlement Service)

BLUE (v3.1) = active
GREEN (v3.2) = standby, fully tested
     LB switches traffic atomically
Risk: LOW | Rollback: Switch LB back
In simple terms: You have two train tracks running in parallel. The train (traffic) is on the blue track. You build and test the green track fully while the train keeps running. When the green track is ready, you switch the points -- the train moves to the green track instantly. If it derails, switch back to blue.
Instructor note: "Why Blue-Green for Settlement and Canary for Transactions?" Settlement is a batch process -- it runs at end of day, it cannot be split 5%/95%. You need to switch it atomically as a whole. Canary works for stateless API traffic that can be load-balanced. This is a great exam-style question.

Act 2 • Why Quality Gates Matter

What Happens Without Gates?

A quality gate is an exit criterion -- a hard check that must pass before the next phase begins. Let's see what happens when teams skip them.

Skipping the Build gate:
Team deploys with known integration test failures ("we'll fix in production"). The circuit breaker code conflicts with the settlement service at runtime. Production goes down at 2 AM during the deployment window. Rollback takes 45 minutes. NOC is overloaded.
Skipping the UAT gate:
Performance test was not run. The PostgreSQL upgrade changed the query planner behavior. The settlement batch now takes 3 hours instead of 29 minutes. Month-end reconciliation misses the RBI deadline.
With gates (our approach):
Build gate catches integration conflicts in QA environment, not production. UAT gate runs 50K TPS load test and catches the PostgreSQL query regression before go-live. The deployment proceeds with confidence because each gate was honestly verified.
Instructor note: "What makes a gate real vs ceremonial?" A real gate has measurable criteria (p99 < 200ms, 0 critical defects). A ceremonial gate is just a checkbox that everyone rubber-stamps. Ask students: what pressure causes teams to turn real gates into ceremonial ones? (Release date pressure, management pressure, "it worked in staging".)

Act 2 • Why Sequence Matters

Deployment Order: Not Arbitrary

Every task in the deployment plan is in its specific position for a reason. Let's look at two critical sequencing decisions.

Why Task 2 (backup) must come before Task 3 (DB upgrade)?

If the PostgreSQL upgrade fails halfway through, the data could be in an inconsistent state. The backup is your only way back. A backup taken after a failed upgrade is useless. "Measure twice, cut once."
Why Task 7 (smoke tests) before Task 8 (canary ramp-up)?

Smoke tests verify that the system is fundamentally alive -- basic transactions work, the API responds, the DB is reachable. Starting a canary ramp-up against a broken system wastes 10-minute monitoring windows and confuses your signals. Confirm the heartbeat before ramping traffic.
Why Task 6 (load balancer config) after Task 4 and 5 (service deployments)?

The load balancer directs traffic to servers. If you update the LB before the new servers are deployed, you route traffic to servers that do not yet have v3.2 -- or worse, to nothing. Always deploy the services, then configure the traffic routing.
Instructor note: Draw this on a whiteboard: LB --> Services --> DB. Traffic flows left to right. Infrastructure changes flow right to left (DB first, then services, then LB). This is the fundamental rule of sequencing in dependent deployments.

Act 2 • In Simple Terms

What Is Canary Deployment?

The name comes from "canary in a coal mine" -- miners sent a canary into the mine first. If the canary survived, it was safe for humans. The canary takes the risk first.

The food tasting analogy: Imagine you are a chef who has made a new dish for a 200-person banquet. Before serving it to everyone, you have one trusted taster try it. If they say "this is great," you serve it to 25 people. If those 25 enjoy it, you serve the whole room. If anyone reports a problem at any stage, you pull the dish and fix it before it reaches everyone. Only 5 people experienced the bad dish -- not 200. 
  04:00   5% traffic to v3.2.0   [monitor 10 min]   GREEN: proceed
  04:10  25% traffic to v3.2.0   [monitor 10 min]   GREEN: proceed
  04:20  50% traffic to v3.2.0   [monitor 10 min]   GREEN: proceed
  04:30 100% traffic to v3.2.0   [deploy to all servers]
Best case: Each stage is GREEN. By 04:30 all traffic is on v3.2.0. No user complained. No rollback needed.
Worst case: At 5% stage, error rate spikes to 0.05%. We redirect traffic back to v3.1 instantly. Only 5% of users saw an error for 10 minutes. v3.1 is fully stable for all users.

Act 3 • In Simple Terms

What Is a PIR?

PIR = Post-Implementation Review. A structured meeting held after every deployment to answer three questions.

Question 1: Did it work?

Compare planned metrics to actual metrics. Did latency improve? Did the settlement batch get faster? Did any user report an issue? This is not about blame -- it is about measuring whether the release delivered what it promised.

Question 2: What went wrong?

Even a successful deployment has surprises. The PostgreSQL stats rebuild took 25 minutes longer than planned. A canary health check fired a false alarm. These are not failures -- they are data. Capture them honestly.

Question 3: What do we do differently next time?

Each issue from Question 2 becomes an action item. "Add 50% buffer to DB migration estimates." "Create separate alert profiles for deployment mode." These action items feed directly into ITIL 4 Continual Improvement.
In simple terms: A PIR is a flight debrief. Pilots review every flight -- not just crashes. What was unexpected? What did the instruments tell us? What will we brief differently next time? A deployment that "went well" still has a PIR, because "went well" should be documented, not assumed.

Teaching Edition • Check for Understanding

Questions to Ask the Class

Use these before moving to Lab 17. If students cannot answer confidently, revisit the relevant slide.

Q1: What is the difference between a Release and a Deployment? Can you have one without the other?

Expected answer: Release = the what and when (the bundle). Deployment = the physical how. Yes -- a release can be approved but not yet deployed. A hotfix can be deployed informally without a release record (bad practice).
Q2: Why do we use Canary for the Transaction Service but Blue-Green for Settlement?

Expected answer: Transactions are stateless API calls -- can be split by percentage at the load balancer. Settlement is a batch process -- it cannot be split. It must switch atomically as a whole unit.
Q3: What is a Blackout Period and why does Diwali week qualify?

Expected answer: A defined period during which no deployments are allowed because the risk of disruption is too high. Diwali sees 300-400% UPI traffic surge. A failed deployment during Diwali would affect millions of transactions.
Q4: In the deployment sequence, why does CHG004 (PostgreSQL) go first?

Expected answer: All other services (Circuit Breaker, Settlement Optimization, Merchant API) depend on the database. If the DB is not upgraded first, the new application code may reference DB features that do not exist yet, causing cascading failures.
