Act 1 • Why It Matters

Why can't Sanjay just use the CMDB?

In simple terms
Think of it like a hospital.

The CMDB is the equipment inventory: "We have 3 MRI machines, 20 ventilators, 50 monitors."

The Service Portfolio is the list of medical services: "We provide Cardiology, Emergency Care, and Radiology."

The equipment supports the services — but knowing you have MRI machines does NOT tell you whether Cardiology is up. You need BOTH.

CMDB alone can't answer:

  • Which services are we offering to customers?
  • Who is accountable if a service fails?
  • Which server failure affects which business service?
  • What does this service cost us?

Service Portfolio answers:

  • "UPI Payment Processing" — owned by Sanjay
  • Criticality 1 — most critical
  • DB failure = this service is impacted
  • Linked to budget codes and contracts

Act 1 • ITIL 4 Concept

The Three Zones — a restaurant analogy

Simple analogy
Imagine a restaurant menu system:

Kitchen (Pipeline): Dishes being developed, not available to customers yet. "We're testing a new dessert recipe."

Active Menu (Catalog): Dishes you can order right now. These are live, available, and priced.

Discontinued Items (Retired): Old items removed from the menu. The recipe still exists in the archives — you just don't serve it anymore.

The Service Portfolio works the same way. It tracks every service — past, present, and future — not just what's live today.

Common misconception: Many people think "Service Portfolio" = "Service Catalog". Not true. The Catalog is just the Catalog zone of the Portfolio — only the currently-live, customer-facing services.

Act 1 • ITIL 4 Concept

What are Service Types — and why do they exist?

In simple terms
Think of a supermarket chain:

Business Service = "Grocery Shopping" — what the customer experiences. They don't care how it works, just that it does.

Technical Service = "Inventory Management System" — internal IT that makes grocery shopping possible. Customers never see this directly.

Application Service = "The specific software instance (SAP WM v3.2) running right now." Very granular — the actual running app.

In ServiceNow, these are separate record types. Keeping them separate lets you answer different questions for different audiences: the VP sees Business Services; the sysadmin sees Technical Services and CIs.

Rule of thumb: If a customer can feel it breaking, it's a Business Service. If only engineers feel it, it's a Technical Service.

Act 2 • Building the Portfolio

Think of it as layers

In simple terms
Like an office building:

Top floor (Business Services): What tenants use — "Meeting Rooms", "Canteen", "Reception". These are what the business cares about.

Middle floor (Technical Services): What building management runs — "HVAC System", "Electrical Grid", "Lift Maintenance". Tenants don't interact with these directly, but they depend on them.

Basement (CIs): Physical equipment — "Boiler Unit 3", "Circuit Breaker Panel A". The actual hardware/software components.

In this lab we're building top-down: start with what the business needs, then link down to the technology that supports it. This is the ITIL way — always start from value.

Why top-down matters: When something breaks, you can answer "which business service is affected?" in seconds — not hours.

Why impact analysis matters — in plain English

In simple terms
Before a Service Portfolio: The DBA calls the on-call engineer at 2 AM: "PostgreSQL is down." The engineer spends 30 minutes calling people, checking dashboards, sending Slack messages — just to understand what is broken for whom.

Act 2 • Step 5

Support tiers — what do L0, L1, L2, L3 actually mean?

In simple terms
Think of it like a hospital emergency system:

L0 (Self-service): "Read the health pamphlet yourself." First-aid kit, FAQs, instructions on the wall. No human involvement needed.

L1 (Service Desk / NOC): "See the triage nurse." Can handle basic issues, take notes, escalate if needed. Cannot do surgery.

L2 (Platform Engineering): "See the general practitioner." Trained to handle most problems, can make config changes and fix app issues.

L3 (Senior Engineering): "See the specialist surgeon." Deep expertise, can fix root causes in code or database. Escalates to the VP (Sanjay) if needed.
Why tiers matter for services: When an incident hits "UPI Payment Processing", ServiceNow knows L1 picks it up first. If unresolved in 30 min, auto-escalate to L2 Amit Verma. Still unresolved? L3 Ravi Kumar. The service record drives this routing — no manual guesswork.

Act 3 • Prove It Works

Why retiring doesn't cascade — explained

In simple terms
Think of it like a shared kitchen in a food court:

The food court has three restaurants: Pizza Place, Burger Joint, and Taco Stand. They all share the same kitchen (the Technical Service).

If Taco Stand closes down (Business Service retired), does the kitchen close? No. Pizza Place and Burger Joint still need it.

Similarly, retiring "UPI Dispute Resolution" does NOT retire "UPI Transaction Processing" — because "UPI Payment Processing" still depends on it.
Before you retire any Business Service, always ask:

1. Which Technical Services does it use?
2. Do other Business Services share those Technical Services?
3. Are there active incidents or SLAs tied to it?
4. Are there open Service Offerings that customers depend on?
The process in ServiceNow:

1. Run Impact Analysis on the Business Service
2. Review all "Used by" relationships
3. Notify downstream teams
4. Change status to Retired
5. Technical Services stay intact
